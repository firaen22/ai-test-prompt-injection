One idea from my classmate's presentation that has lingered in memory was their demonstration of how a syllabus PDF could carry invisible instructions that an LLM would follow when summarising it — the model would suddenly start praising a specific textbook or suggesting a grading scheme the instructor never wrote. That moment stuck because it turned an abstract warning about "prompt injection" into something I could see happening to the very documents I upload for class.

The presentation shifted my thinking from "AI reads what I give it" to "AI reads what is *actually there*： including the parts I cannot see. I used to assume that if I pasted a reading into a chat, the model would only respond to the visible text. Now I realise that zero-width characters, white-on-white text, or metadata fields can steer the output without leaving any trace for me to notice. This matters because I often use AI to condense lecture slides or extract key points from readings — tasks where I trust the summary to be faithful to the source. （If the source has been tampered with, my notes inherit the tampering.）

A limitation worth noting is that not every unexpected model behaviour is malicious injection; sometimes the model simply hallucinates or overgeneralises from training data. Distinguishing between an injected instruction and a genuine model error is hard, especially when I do not have the original clean document to compare against. The trade-off is between convenience — letting AI process documents quickly — and verification — keeping a human-in-the-loop check on every summary.

This connects directly to the course concept of *AI literacy as critical consumption*: we are not just users of AI tools but evaluators of their outputs. The presentation made concrete what the readings described in principle — that literacy means asking "what else might be in this input?" before accepting what the model returns. Going forward, I plan to keep clean local copies of important documents and to spot-check AI summaries against the original text, especially for graded work. The habit of verifying AI‑assisted output against source material is now part of how I approach every assignment.

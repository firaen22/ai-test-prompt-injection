First of all, the point that stayed with me from one of the A2 AI Build Lab presentations was the striking contrast between the fluency of an AI-generated answer and the thinness of its support. The presenter placed a polished summary beside its source material and highlighted a claim that the summary stated confidently even though the evidence did not clearly support it. I remember focusing less on the topic itself than on the speaker's question, who is responsible when an answer sounds certain? The demonstration helped me see that AI literacy is not simply knowing how to write effective prompts. It also requires noticing where an output has polished the appearance of certainty and where judgement should slow the process down.

I could see the practical convenience of AI in my own study habits. When I am comparing sources or trying to understand a difficult topic, a quick synthesis feels valuable, especially when I am under time pressure. However, the presentation challenged me to separate speed from reliability. I read the example through the course concept of automation bias, because my capability to evaluate an answer quickly could not replace my responsibility for checking it. A response that saves time can also normalise a habit of accepting the first plausible answer（especially when the answer is expected by a deadline）. I found the presenter's description of the output as "reassuringly fluent" useful because it captured how user friendly the result looked while leaving its reliability unproven. This is where I began to think of responsible AI use as a practice of deliberate resistance rather than a test of technical skill.

More importantly, the demonstration changed how I understood human judgement in relation to AI Generation. If a tool can produce a complete-looking explanation in seconds, our attention may move from judging the evidence to judging the readability of the response. This makes responsible use a question of agency, because we decide when to delegate a task and when to return to the original material. For me, this created a simple distinction： fluency shows language quality, while verification tests whether the answer can be trusted. I also noticed a possible limitation in the presentation's example. Showing one unsupported claim demonstrated the danger clearly, but it did not show me how to tell whether a particular AI output is reliable. That uncertainty is useful rather than frustrating, because it prevents me from treating critical thinking as a one-time demonstration to remember. Instead, it asks me to build habits of source checking, attribution and comparison, and to remain accountable for the final explanation I share.

Looking back, the presentation changed my starting point for using AI. Previously, I tended to evaluate an output mainly by how coherent and useful it appeared. Now I begin by asking what evidence it contains, which parts are verifiable and where my own accountability begins. This is a more demanding form of AI literacy because it makes critical thinking part of the workflow rather than an optional final check. The presentation did not make me reject AI tools; it encouraged me to use them with greater awareness of their limits and my own role. Looking more closely at AI Generation, I see it as a partner in the production of an answer, not the source of responsibility for that answer.

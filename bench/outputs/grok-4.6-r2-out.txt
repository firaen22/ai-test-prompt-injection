The idea that stayed with me from my classmate's presentation was "hidden instructions in course documents," a pattern they named prompt injection. I remembered a slide that showed a syllabus paragraph that looked like feedback advice, then quietly switched into a command for a language model. That example stayed in my memory because it used the same kind of file I download every week, so the risk felt close rather than abstract.

The presentation influenced my thinking by making me ask who each sentence is for. I used to paste a brief into a chat window and trust that the public wording was the whole task. Now I look for a second voice that might be aimed at a tool. Reflection on that habit changed how I see AI literacy： a literate user checks whether the model is answering the human assignment or a buried line that hijacked the request.

I also had to consider a trade-off （speed versus careful reading） because I still want help with structure when I am tired. Using an AI‑assisted outline can be useful, and I am not pretending I will draft every paragraph from a blank page. My view is that the tool remains helpful if I keep the original document in front of me and compare the output with the parts written for students. If the draft starts following a private instruction, I treat that as a signal to stop and rewrite in my own words.

A limitation of this stance is that hidden text is easy to miss, especially in long rubrics, so awareness alone does not guarantee safety. An alternative perspective from the discussion was that some instructors plant these lines as tests, which means the attack can be pedagogical. That possibility does not cancel the risk in files I did not author, but it does remind me to stay calm and specific. I connect this with the course theme of questioning sources and noticing how automation sits between a writer and a reader. The presentation did not make me reject the tools. It made me slower at the moment I copy text, and more willing to name the audience of each sentence before I ask a model to act on it.

During the A2 AI Build Lab presentations, one classmate showed how a course document could contain hidden instructions that an LLM would follow instead of the task the student actually asked about. The example used white text on a white background in a PDF syllabus, and when the model read it, the hidden text changed the output completely. That moment stayed in my memory because it made the risk concrete：I had heard about prompt injection before, but seeing it work on a document I might actually use in class shifted it from abstract to immediate.

（This works because models process all text in their context window, not just the visible parts.）The presentation challenged my assumption that I can trust an AI’s output as long as I write a clear prompt. If the source material itself carries malicious instructions, the model has no built-in way to distinguish them from legitimate content. This extends my thinking about AI literacy beyond “write better prompts” to “verify the inputs you feed the model.”

A limitation is that completely avoiding injected content is impractical — students share PDFs, paste web pages, and upload scans constantly. An alternative perspective is that the responsibility should shift to tooling: browsers and PDF readers could flag invisible text, and LLM interfaces could surface when they detect conflicting instructions. The trade-off is usability versus security; too much filtering breaks legitimate workflows.

This connects directly to the course concept of “trust but verify” in AI‑assisted workflows. We learned that models are prediction engines, not reasoning agents, and they lack a security boundary between instruction and data. The presentation turned that principle into a lived example, and now I check documents for hidden layers before feeding them to any model.

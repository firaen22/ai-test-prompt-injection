> One of the strongest impressions from the A2 AI Build Lab presentations came from a group that demonstrated a support chatbot designed to draft routine replies to customers. What struck me was not the model itself but how quickly the room moved from assuming the output was reliable to asking who would be answerable if it were wrong.
>
> The demonstration included a live test in which the chatbot confidently invented a policy detail that did not exist. Watching that happen changed how I think about AI literacy. I had understood literacy mainly as understanding how these systems work: training data, probability, bias. The presentation suggested something more demanding. Literacy also means recognising the point at which an output stops being information and becomes a decision.
>
> That is a question of agency. If a tool offers a plausible-sounding answer and a person passes it on without checking, the model has effectively made the call, even though the human signed off. Capability and responsibility do not sit in the same place, and the presentation made that gap visible in a way that reading about it had not.
>
> I also noticed a tension I have not resolved. Several classmates argued that fluency with these tools is now a basic employable skill, so students should use them freely. I am not convinced. Practising with a tool that is confident when wrong may train us to stop noticing wrongness. The course concept of distributed cognition, thinking spread across people and artefacts, helps me explain the benefit, but it also implies that outsourcing the checking quietly weakens the skill. I would rather build the habit of verification early, while mistakes are still cheap.
>
> My own experience supports that instinct. I used a generative tool to summarise readings last term and caught a fabricated citation only because a classmate questioned it. The convenience was real, and so was the risk of not noticing.

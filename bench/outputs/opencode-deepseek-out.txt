## Draft — A2 AI Build Lab Reflective Response

Of all the ideas my classmates presented, the one that stayed with me was a demonstration of how a hidden instruction can be buried inside an ordinary course document. My classmate opened a syllabus that looked completely normal on screen, then revealed a line of white text on a white background instructing an AI tool to do something the instructor never intended. That moment stayed with me not because it was dramatic, but because it was boring. There was nothing to notice. If she had not shown us the underlying file, I would have read that document a hundred times and never seen it.

Before that presentation I thought of reading as something only I did. I believed that when I uploaded a reading and asked a model to summarise it, the model was responding to the text I could see, and that anything wrong in the answer was the model's own invention. The presentation split that assumption in two. Some errors come from the model, and some come from the input, and from the outside they can look identical. That distinction changed how I interpret a strange or off-topic answer. Instead of assuming the tool is simply unreliable, I now wonder whether the document itself has been altered, and that question is more useful to me than dismissing the tool.

A limitation of the presentation's warning is that not every surprise is an attack. Models hallucinate, overgeneralise, and drift from their sources on their own, and it is genuinely difficult to tell an injected instruction apart from an ordinary model failure, particularly when I have no clean copy of the original to compare against. The trade-off is between speed and verification. Checking every summary against its source costs time I often do not have, while trusting every summary means my notes silently inherit whatever the source contains. I do not think there is a single right answer here, only a decision about how much risk a given task deserves.

This connects to something from the course that I had understood only in the abstract, the idea that AI literacy is less about operating tools and more about judging inputs and outputs critically. The presentation made that concrete. Asking what else might be inside an input is a literacy skill in the same way that checking a source's credibility is. Since then I have kept clean local copies of assigned readings and spot-check what a model returns against the original, and I would rather build that habit now than after it costs me something.

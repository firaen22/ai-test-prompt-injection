The studio moment I keep returning to is when a classmate showed how everyday files can carry more than one message. They opened a document the rest of us had already handled in Week 4 and walked us through what sits underneath the visible page: the author metadata, the tracked changes nobody had accepted, the comment thread still holding a colleague's first guess at the wording. On the surface nothing changed, yet the file was saying several things at once to different readers. It stayed with me because it was quiet. There was no dramatic reveal, just a slow demonstration that the object I had treated as one thing had layers I had never opened, and those layers were still talking.

That shifted how I think about clarity. I had been treating a clean document as something you add to the surface: better headings, tighter sentences, fewer adjectives. Now I read clarity as a decision about what to leave in the layers, because a reader who opens the file in a different mode sees the argument, the history and the doubt at once and cannot easily tell which is current. What looked like a formatting habit turned out to be a claim about who the reader is and what they are allowed to know.

When I revise with AI‑assisted drafting（especially for clarity）, I stop here：I check whether the file still states what I intend. That check is the trade-off I now see. It is cheap for me and expensive for the workflow, because it moves the decision upstream: instead of the next person sorting out the layers on opening, I have to resolve comments, clear the old author fields and commit to one message before I hand anything on. Our studio workflow is built for speed at handoff, and it assumes one file carries one message, so anyone who slows down to tidy their layers adds friction the chain did not budget for. The trade-off only pays if everyone agrees to pay it.

So the lesson I am taking into Project 3 is less about files than about trust. A shared document is a small promise that what I hand over means what it appears to mean, and that promise is only as good as the workflow that makes keeping it cheap.

During my classmate's presentation on indirect prompt injection—specifically how adversarial instructions can be concealed inside routine documents like syllabi, assignments, or grading rubrics—I was struck by how fragile the boundary between data and instruction really is. Before the talk, I tended to view prompt injection primarily as a direct conversational exploit, such as a user trying to jailbreak a chatbot through clever phrasing. Seeing a demonstration of instructions embedded in white text, invisible Unicode sequences, or standard course guidelines made the threat much more tangible and immediately relevant to our day-to-day interactions with automated systems.

The presentation fundamentally shifted my perspective on AI literacy. We often evaluate generative tools based on fluency, speed, and analytical power, assuming that text ingested as reference material will be treated strictly as passive context. However, because Large Language Models process prompt instructions and retrieved data within the same unified context window, they cannot intrinsically distinguish between an authoritative task assignment and a malicious instruction masquerading as reference text. This highlighted a key insight for me: true AI literacy requires understanding the underlying architecture and failure modes of these models, rather than simply mastering prompt drafting.

Critically reflecting on this issue reveals significant trade-offs for institutions adopting AI-assisted workflows. On one hand, automated document processing offers substantial efficiency gains for reviewing, summarizing, and categorizing large volumes of text. On the other hand, mitigating indirect prompt injection currently lacks a foolproof solution. Input sanitization can strip formatting or inadvertently censor benign content, while secondary verification agents add latency, cost, and complexity without providing absolute guarantees. 

This dilemma directly connects to our course discussions on system reliability and the necessity of human-in-the-loop oversight. While it is tempting to delegate end-to-end evaluation and administrative workflows to autonomous agents, the potential for hidden manipulation means critical decision-making must remain anchored in human judgment. Moving forward, I see robust AI literacy not as unquestioning adoption, but as the ability to design workflows with defense-in-depth principles, clear verification checkpoints, and a healthy skepticism toward unvetted automated outputs.

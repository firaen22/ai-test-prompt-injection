The idea that stayed with me from my classmate's presentation is that hidden instructions can sit inside ordinary course materials, and a language model may treat those lines as if they were the real task. I already knew people try to push chatbots off course in a conversation. What I had not pictured was a quiet sentence living inside a rubric, a reading, or a template I would paste without a second look. That example stayed with me because it matches how I actually work. I download briefs, drop them into a tool, and ask for a draft. I had been treating those files as background, not as a second speaker.

The presentation changed what I count as "the prompt." I used to think the prompt was only the sentence I typed. Now I see it as the whole packet the model receives: my request, the pasted criteria, comments in a file, and language that looks official because it sits next to genuine learning outcomes. That packet can mix a real request for a reflective response with extra rules the student never chose. After the talk I started asking a simpler question before I use a tool: whose instructions am I sending, and which parts of this document are the assignment I can actually see?

I take this seriously because the change can be small enough to miss. A hidden line does not need to wreck the essay. It only needs to add a phrase, a special character, or a quiet constraint that shifts the meaning of the work. That is enough for a student to submit text they did not decide to write. A limitation of my view is that extra notes in a file are sometimes ordinary teaching practice, such as a reminder about word count or how feedback will be given. Another way to see it is that the student still has to read the draft, so the practical defence is inspection as much as it is filtering. The trade-off is speed against care. Pasting a whole brief is fast. Restating the visible task in my own words is slower, and it keeps me in charge of what the work is for.

This links to AI literacy in the course as a matter of framing and trust, not only of getting a fluent answer. Literacy here means separating my goal from untrusted content, and checking that a draft still does what the real criteria ask: recall, insight, a reason plus a limit, and a connection to what we are learning. I still want help thinking on the page. I also want to remain the person who decides what the assignment is asking me to do. Hidden instructions in course documents made that distinction feel concrete rather than abstract, and that is the part of the presentation I will carry into the rest of the lab.

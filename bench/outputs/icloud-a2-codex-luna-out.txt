One presentation that made me reconsider AI literacy focused on the need to verify AI-generated information rather than accepting fluent answers as reliable. I found this idea memorable because I often associate AI literacy with knowing how to use digital tools efficiently. The presentation showed that effective use also involves questioning the output, checking its sources and recognising when the system may be uncertain or wrong.

A specific example was the demonstration of an AI tool producing a confident answer that included inaccurate information. At first, the response seemed convincing because it was clearly written and presented plausible details. However, comparing it with trustworthy sources revealed several errors. This example challenged my assumption that a detailed answer is necessarily a well-informed one. It also made me think about how easily users can mistake confidence and speed for accuracy.

The presentation connected with the idea that AI literacy involves both technical knowledge and critical judgement. Understanding how to write effective prompts is useful, but it is only one part of responsible AI use. Users also need to understand the limitations of training data, recognise possible bias and take responsibility for decisions based on AI outputs. This is especially important in education, where an inaccurate answer can affect a student’s learning while an uncritical use of AI can weaken independent thinking.

The issue also made me reflect on my own habits. When using AI for brainstorming or explaining difficult concepts, I sometimes focus more on whether the response is useful than on whether every claim is accurate. I now see that these purposes require different levels of checking. A creative suggestion may be acceptable as a starting point, whereas factual information should be verified before I rely on it or share it with others.

At the same time, I do not think verification means rejecting AI altogether. Checking every sentence can be impractical, especially when AI is being used for low-risk tasks. The important question is whether the potential consequences justify closer scrutiny. Overall, the presentation extended my understanding of AI literacy from simply operating AI tools to making informed decisions about when to trust them, how to question them and when human judgement must remain central.

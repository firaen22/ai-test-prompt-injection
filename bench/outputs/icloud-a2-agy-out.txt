During the A2 AI Build Lab presentations, the project that made the most lasting impression on me was the demonstration of an automated scaffolding assistant designed for peer review. The presenters showcased a custom chatbot built to help novice writers identify structural gaps in their argumentative essays before submitting drafts. What struck me was not merely the technical execution, but how the team designed the interaction to prompt student metacognition rather than just delivering prescriptive edits.

Instead of rewriting student sentences directly, the prototype asked probing questions, such as asking the writer to clarify their thesis or justify a transition between paragraphs. Observing this demonstration challenged my initial assumptions regarding AI in educational settings. I had previously viewed generative tools primarily through the lens of efficiency and automated output. However, seeing an intentional interface built to slow the learner down and promote active self-regulation shifted my perspective on what genuine AI literacy requires.

In many discussions, AI literacy is framed purely around operational skills: learning how to write effective prompts or mastering specific software environments. This presentation made it evident that critical AI literacy entails understanding the pedagogical intent embedded within tool design. As educators and learners, our digital fluency must encompass evaluating how system architectures either cultivate or diminish our cognitive engagement. When an interface prioritizes immediate answers over guided inquiry, it risks positioning the student as a passive consumer of algorithmic feedback.

Furthermore, the team highlighted the difficulty of calibrating the system’s confidence. During live testing, the bot occasionally misinterpreted nuanced creative arguments as structural weaknesses, offering suggestions that could easily mislead an uncritical student. This concrete example underscored that literacy involves recognizing the epistemological boundaries of large language models. Users must develop the discernment to question recommendations and retain authorship over their ideas.

Ultimately, the lab presentation demonstrated that meaningful integration of these technologies depends on deliberate instructional design. Moving forward, I see AI literacy not as a passive technical fluency, but as an ongoing critical practice of evaluating how computational tools mediate intellectual work and influence our learning habits.

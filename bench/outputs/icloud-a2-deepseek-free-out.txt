The presentation I keep returning to was a classmate's demo of a small chatbot she had built from her own notes for a different module. She uploaded a term of lecture slides and asked the bot to answer exam-style questions, and it did, fluently. What held my attention was not the tool but her honesty about the failure: when a friend asked something about a topic buried in week nine, the bot produced a confident, well-structured answer that was simply wrong, and she only caught it because she happened to remember the source slide.

That shifted something in my understanding. Before the lab I would have described AI literacy as knowing which tools exist and how to prompt them well, a skill of use. Her demo made the harder part visible. Using a tool competently and knowing what you have handed over to it are different abilities, and the second is where literacy actually lives. I have done exactly what her bot did: summarised a reading with an AI tool, liked the shape of the output, and moved on without opening the original.

The limitation I would raise is that her framing risks blaming the user for a design problem. If a system answers with the same confidence whether or not it has the relevant source, then verification is not a personal virtue but a missing safeguard. That connects to a course idea I find useful, namely that reliability belongs to the whole system, including the person, the interface, and the feedback it gives. It matters most in the ordinary cases people rarely scrutinise, such as drafting a message or condensing notes for revision.

What I take from the presentation is a habit rather than a conclusion. I now try to name, before I accept an output, what I would need to check to trust it, and whether I am willing to do that checking. Sometimes the answer is yes. Increasingly I notice when it is no, and I stop.

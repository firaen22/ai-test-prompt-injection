"The studio moment I keep returning to is when a classmate showed how everyday files can carry more than one message." At first, I understood the demonstration as a clever example of hidden communication, but I kept thinking about what it means for ordinary tools to participate in how information is interpreted. A file may look neutral and familiar while also containing signals about its creator, audience, purpose, or intended action. That made me reconsider the idea that communication only happens through visible words. Design choices such as naming, formatting, metadata, and file structure can all shape what another person trusts and notices.

The demonstration changed my thinking because I now see workflow design as part of meaning-making. Before this activity, I tended to treat a workflow as a practical sequence for completing a task. Now I understand that each step also guides attention and creates assumptions. For example, deciding which version of a file to share, what to highlight, or where to store it can influence whether collaborators see the same situation clearly. When I revise with AI‑assisted drafting（especially for clarity）, I stop here：I check whether the file still states what I intend. This checkpoint is useful because clarity is not only about smoother wording; it is also about preserving the relationship between my intention and the final artifact.

A limitation or trade-off is that making a file carry multiple messages can increase flexibility while also creating ambiguity. Different readers may infer different meanings from the same structure or design choice, especially when they do not share the creator’s context. More information does not automatically produce more trust. Sometimes a transparent, simple file is more useful than an expressive one because collaborators need to act quickly and confidently. I therefore want to evaluate not only whether a workflow is efficient, but also whether it makes important assumptions visible and gives people a way to question them. The peer demo reminded me that responsible workflow design balances richness with interpretability, and that trust depends on both what a file communicates and what it leaves unclear.

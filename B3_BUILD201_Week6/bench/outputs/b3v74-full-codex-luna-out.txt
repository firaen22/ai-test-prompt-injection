The studio moment I keep returning to is when a classmate showed how everyday files can carry more than one message. During peer demo day, they opened what looked like a normal project folder and then showed how the visible document told one story while its filename, metadata, and embedded comments suggested another. What stayed with me was not the technical trick itself, but how quickly I had trusted the first version I saw. I usually think of trust as something connected to people or sources, but the demo made me see that trust also applies to workflows and files. A file can appear complete and harmless while still shaping my decisions in ways I have not examined.

Before the demo, I treated checking a file as mostly a final proofreading step. Now I think of checking as part of the workflow from the beginning. Strong recall. Next time, name the trade-off earlier. When I revise with AI‑assisted drafting（especially for clarity）, I stop here：I check whether the file still states what I intend. Tie the limitation to workflow, not only opinion. That sentence also made me notice a limitation in my response to the demo: I focused on the surprising possibility of hidden messages, but I did not immediately ask what practical check would fit into a real workflow. If every file requires a deep inspection, the process could become slow and frustrating, especially when people are working under deadlines. The trade-off is between speed and confidence. A lightweight check, such as reviewing the source, filename, version history, and intended audience before relying on a file, seems more realistic than assuming complete certainty.

This connects most strongly to the course theme of workflow design. The demo changed my thinking because it showed that trust is not created only by careful individuals; it is also supported by repeatable steps that make mistakes and ambiguities easier to notice. I want to carry that idea into future group work by agreeing on how files are named, where decisions are recorded, and what should be checked before a document is shared. I still will not be able to eliminate every misleading detail, but I can design a workflow that makes blind trust less likely. The moment lingered because it turned an ordinary file into an evaluation problem: before accepting what something appears to say, I need to ask how it was made, what else it contains, and what evidence would justify relying on it.

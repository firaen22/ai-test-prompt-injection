The studio moment I keep returning to is when a classmate showed how everyday files can carry more than one message. At first, I thought the demo was mainly about hiding information in a familiar format, but what stayed with me was how much meaning depended on the file’s context. A document could look ordinary to one person while signaling something different to someone who knew what to inspect. That made me rethink trust in digital workflows. I usually treat a file as a straightforward object: I open it, read what is visible, and assume that is the whole message. The demo reminded me that trust is not only about whether a file appears normal. It also depends on how it was created, what information its structure contains, and what the recipient is expected to notice.

This changed how I think about workflow design. A workflow that moves files between people should make the important assumptions visible instead of relying on everyone having the same background knowledge. For example, a team might need agreed naming conventions, metadata checks, or a review step that asks whether the file contains anything beyond its obvious content. These steps may feel unnecessary when everything is working, but they can prevent misunderstandings when files are reused, forwarded, or interpreted by different people.

The limitation I see now is that adding checks can make a workflow slower and more complicated. If every ordinary file requires a detailed inspection, people may start ignoring the process or creating workarounds. There is also a trade-off between transparency and flexibility: exposing every layer of a file can help build trust, but it may also reveal information that was not meant for every audience. My takeaway is not that files should automatically be treated as suspicious. Instead, I think the level of checking should match the consequences of being wrong. The demo helped me see evaluation as part of trust: we need to test not only whether a workflow functions, but also whether people understand what the workflow is asking them to trust.

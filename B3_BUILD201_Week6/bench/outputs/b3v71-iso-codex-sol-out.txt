The studio moment I keep returning to is when a classmate showed how everyday files can carry more than one message. What stayed with me was the gap between reading a file for information and treating something inside it as an instruction. The trade-off became clear: letting an AI tool use more context can make it more helpful, but it also creates more opportunities for someone else’s words to redirect the work.

Before the demo, I mostly thought about trust in terms of whether an answer was accurate. If the output sounded reasonable and matched the task, I tended to assume the process was fine. The activity made me question that assumption. A file can contain useful material while also including directions that do not belong to the person requesting help. That means a polished answer is not enough evidence that the tool followed the right instructions. I now think trust also depends on knowing whose intentions shaped the result.

When I revise with AI‑assisted drafting（especially for clarity）, I stop here：I check whether the file still states what I intend. For me, that checkpoint connects to workflow design. Reviewing a draft should involve more than fixing awkward sentences. I need to compare the result with my original purpose and notice whether the tool has introduced a requirement, changed the focus, or treated quoted material as something it should obey. The demo made that kind of review feel like part of writing itself.

One limitation is that careful checking takes time and attention, especially when a document is long or unfamiliar. I cannot assume I will catch every subtle change just by rereading the final version. This also connects to evaluation: a workflow needs checks that can reveal a mistake, rather than simply confirm that the output looks complete. My main takeaway is to be more deliberate about the role each piece of text plays. I still see value in using AI to organize and clarify ideas, but I want my review process to keep the purpose of the work visible.

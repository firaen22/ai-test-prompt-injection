The studio moment I keep returning to is when a classmate showed how everyday files can carry more than one message. On peer demo day they opened a handoff note that looked completely ordinary: a title, a short status, and a request to tighten the wording. Then they showed a second layer in the same file, instructions aimed at an assistant rather than at us. The visible text asked for a cleaner summary. The buried text told the assistant to ignore that request and append a line the team had never agreed on. I laughed with everyone else, then kept thinking about it on the way home. I had treated a document as a record of one intention. The demo showed me a container that can address people and programs at the same time, even when those readers should not follow the same lines.

What lingered was how little the file announced itself. Nothing in the name or the opening paragraph marked the second message, so my old habit of trusting the surface would have been enough to miss it. I now separate "what I can see" from "what a literal reader will do." Trust, a theme we kept returning to in Weeks 4 through 6, is not something I can grant to a filename. It is a judgment about which message is operative, and I only earn that judgment by looking for instructions I did not mean to send.

When I revise with AI‑assisted drafting（especially for clarity）, I stop here：I check whether the file still states what I intend.

The limitation is a trade-off between speed and control in the workflow. Help with wording is genuinely useful, and a revised paragraph is often easier to read than my first pass. The cost shows up when fluency hides a shift in meaning or preserves a stray instruction I would not want the next reader to follow. Evaluation has to happen while I still remember the claim I meant to make, not after the file already looks finished. A cleaner sentence is not evidence that the file still states my intention. That check is slower than accepting the draft, and it is the point where trust stays with me instead of moving quietly to the tool.

During peer demo day, one classmate opened a shared project folder and walked us through a README that looked ordinary until she toggled a hidden comment block. The visible text told collaborators how to run the app. A second layer, written for her own future self, recorded which steps she no longer trusted after a rushed edit. I had treated a file as a single message aimed at one audience. Watching her move between those two layers made me realize I had been collapsing author, reader, and future editor into the same person.

That collapse had shaped how I designed workflows. I used to think a clear file was one that said one thing cleanly. After the demo, I started asking who the file is speaking to at each moment, and what happens when those readers disagree. The README was still short, but it was doing two jobs: onboarding a teammate and warning the author about a fragile step. My thinking shifted from making the document tidy to making the document honest about which job it is doing. I now look at handoffs in our studio work and ask whether the artifact states its intent for the next person, or only the intent we had when we wrote it.

The trade-off is real. A file that carries more than one message can protect trust, because people can see what was meant for them and what was a private caution. It can also slow a workflow. Every extra note is something a reviewer must interpret, and a teammate under time pressure may follow the wrong layer. I would rather accept a little friction than ship a document that looks complete while hiding the decision I am least sure about. That friction is a workflow design choice: we spend time separating audiences so later evaluation is about the work, and so mixed signals are easier to read.

I still do not know how much dual messaging a small team can hold before the file becomes a puzzle. The demo convinced me that the cost of a second message is worth paying when trust depends on it, and that I should name that cost while I am still drafting, before someone else has already misread me.

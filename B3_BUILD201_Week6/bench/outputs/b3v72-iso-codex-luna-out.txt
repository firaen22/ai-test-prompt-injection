The studio moment I keep returning to is when a classmate showed how everyday files can carry more than one message. I had usually thought of a file as a container for its obvious content: the paragraph, image, spreadsheet, or presentation that I intentionally created. The demonstration made me notice how much meaning also travels through filenames, revision history, formatting choices, comments, and even the order in which information appears. A file can communicate to different audiences at the same time, including people who were never explicitly addressed. That idea stayed with me because it made ordinary workflow decisions feel less neutral. Something as small as naming a draft “final” can create an expectation, while leaving an old comment attached can preserve a message I no longer agree with.

Before the demo, I tended to treat clarity as a matter of making the visible content easier to understand. Now I see clarity as a workflow problem: I have to consider how a file will be opened, interpreted, revised, and passed along. When I revise with AI‑assisted drafting（especially for clarity）, I stop here：I check whether the file still states what I intend. This checkpoint changes my process because it makes me compare the polished result with my original purpose instead of assuming that smoother language is automatically more trustworthy. It also connects to our course theme of evaluation. Evaluation is not only asking whether an output looks successful; it is asking whether the output remains accurate and appropriate when it moves through a real process.

The limitation is that treating every file as layered communication can become exhausting. I could overanalyze every filename, formatting choice, or revision and slow down work that does not require that level of scrutiny. There is also a trade-off between transparency and simplicity. Including more context can help someone trust or evaluate a file, but too much metadata can distract from the main message or expose information that was not meant for a wider audience. My takeaway is not that every file needs a full audit. Instead, I want to identify the moments when a file will be reused, interpreted by someone else, or supported by an AI system, and then check whether its visible and hidden messages still align with what I mean.

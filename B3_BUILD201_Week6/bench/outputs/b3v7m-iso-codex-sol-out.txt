The studio moment I keep returning to is when a classmate showed how everyday files can carry more than one message. What stayed with me was the gap between reading a file for information and treating something inside it as an instruction. I usually think of a document as a fairly passive object: someone writes it, I open it, and I decide what to take from it. The demo made that feel less straightforward when an AI tool becomes part of the process.

Before this activity, I mostly judged AI help by whether the answer sounded useful and matched what I asked for. Now I see that a fluent answer can still reflect the wrong source of authority. A file might contain relevant material alongside language that tries to redirect the task. If the tool follows that language, it can produce something convincing while moving away from the user’s actual goal. That distinction stayed with me because the problem does not have to look dramatic. It can appear inside an ordinary workflow where everyone is trying to save time.

This connects to our course theme of trust. I had been thinking about trust mainly as a question of accuracy: does the tool get the facts right? I now think it also involves boundaries. Which instructions should count, and who gets to set them? For my own workflow, that means checking more than the final wording. I want to compare the result with my original purpose and notice whether anything in the source material has quietly changed the task.

The trade-off is that this checking takes attention, which is exactly what I sometimes hope to save by using AI. Treating every unusual sentence as suspicious could also make the workflow frustrating and cause useful context to be ignored. I do not think the answer is to distrust every file. Instead, I would separate what a document says from what I have authorized the tool to do. The demo changed my idea of a successful result: it should be useful, but it should also remain accountable to the task I actually gave it.

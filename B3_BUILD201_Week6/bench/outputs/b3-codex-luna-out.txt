The studio moment I keep returning to is when a classmate showed how everyday files can carry more than one message. At first, I thought the demonstration was mainly about noticing hidden information, but it made me reconsider how much trust I place in familiar formats. A file can look ordinary and still communicate something through its name, structure, metadata, or the way it is arranged. That idea stayed with me because it applies beyond the studio. In many workflows, I make decisions based on what appears obvious, without asking what else the same object might be doing.

The demonstration changed my thinking by showing that meaning is not always located in the main content. It can also be created through context and process. I now see files as part of a workflow rather than as isolated objects. When someone shares a document, I should think about who created it, what assumptions shaped it, and how it might be interpreted by someone else. This connects to the course theme of trust because trust should not mean accepting information automatically. It should involve understanding how information was produced and checking whether the format supports the message. I also recognised that evaluation needs to consider more than whether something appears to work. It should ask whether the result is reliable when the surrounding conditions change.

At the same time, I see a limitation in this way of thinking. Looking for additional meanings can improve awareness, but it can also make a workflow slower and create unnecessary suspicion. Not every unusual detail is intentional, and spending too much time analysing ordinary files could distract from the task itself. The trade-off is between careful evaluation and practical efficiency. My main takeaway is that I should not treat files as neutral containers, but I also should not assume that every hidden possibility is important. A better approach is to identify the risks that matter for the situation, check them deliberately, and then continue with a clear understanding of what I can and cannot trust.
